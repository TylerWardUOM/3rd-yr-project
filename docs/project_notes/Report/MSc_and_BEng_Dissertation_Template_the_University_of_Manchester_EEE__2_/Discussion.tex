%Discussion section 
This section interprets the main constraints on haptic performance: communication latency, delayed contact behaviour, hardware limitations, and design trade-offs. The emphasis is on what the results allow the system to claim, what they do not yet support, and how these limitations motivate the future work in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.

\subsection{Communication and Latency Constraints} \label{sec:discussion_communication_latency}

The central finding from the communication evaluation is that transport delay, measured at 15.60--15.67\,ms mean round-trip with 95th and 99th percentiles around 16.2--16.4\,ms, is the dominant timing limitation. Host-side processing latency was much lower (mean 0.299\,ms, 99th percentile 2.04\,ms), so computation was not the bottleneck for the nominal 1\,kHz local haptic/device loops. This delay bounds achievable stiffness because phase lag reduces stability margin and can inject artificial energy into the sampled-data loop \cite{colgatePassivityClassSampleddata1997}; the realised system therefore renders compliant rather than rigid contact.

The ST-Link virtual COM interface was convenient, but the burst-like delivery pattern and packet loss at low transmission rates suggest buffering and scheduling overhead somewhere in the USB virtual-COM path, including the ST-Link interface, Windows serial stack, and host-side scheduling. The present measurements identify the path-level limitation but do not isolate which stage contributes most delay. The system should therefore be interpreted as a high-rate local renderer operating over delayed sampled state, rather than as a true 1\,kHz end-to-end haptic loop. Snapshot channels prevent stale-state backlog, but cannot remove transport delay or guarantee 1\,ms physical state freshness. This directly motivates the future communication work in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}, where the ST-Link path should be compared against lower-latency transport options before stronger responsiveness claims are made.

\subsection{Local Contact State Limitation} \label{sec:discussion_local_contact_state}

The nominal 1\,kHz haptic loop did not evolve a complete local contact state independently of slower simulation and communication layers. It consumed latest-available snapshots, then recomputed proxy/contact information each cycle. The renderer could execute at high frequency, but the physical meaning of each update was still bounded by snapshot freshness and communication delay.

A stronger architecture would maintain persistent proxy and local contact state inside the high-rate loop. Rather than re-projecting the proxy from scratch, the proxy would evolve continuously subject to contact constraints, preserving contact history, tangential motion, and release behaviour. This is closer to constraint-based proxy or god-object approaches \cite{zillesConstraintbasedGodobjectMethod1995,ruspiniHapticDisplayComplex1997,salisburyHapticRenderingSurfaces1997} and to multilevel haptic rendering, where slow global updates are combined with a fast local model \cite{ruspiniHapticDisplayComplex1997}. This limitation explains why future work should move either the full haptic renderer or a reduced local contact model closer to the device, rather than only increasing host-side update frequency.

\subsection{Observed Contact Behaviour Under Delay} \label{sec:discussion_stability}

Despite imperfect communication timing, the tested interactions remained bounded because snapshot channels used the newest available state, thread separation avoided cross-subsystem blocking, damping countered delayed feedback, and embedded torque rate limiting prevented abrupt torque transitions. Watchdog activation also removed torque during communication interruptions (Section~\ref{sec:results}). These choices were effective for a prototype because they favoured conservative behaviour over maximum stiffness.

These mechanisms address the passivity concerns identified by Colgate and Schenkel \cite{colgatePassivityClassSampleddata1997} in a practical rather than formal way. The results show bounded behaviour in the tested cases, not a passivity proof. Contact remained controllable without divergent motion, and this is an important result given the measured 15--16\,ms communication delay. However, the compliant feel was a direct consequence of delayed feedback; rigid contact cannot be achieved under the measured latency conditions by force-model tuning alone. The future-work route is therefore not simply to increase virtual stiffness, but to reduce effective delay, maintain local contact state, and add calibrated sensing so that stability and force fidelity can be assessed quantitatively.

\subsection{Hardware Limitations} \label{sec:discussion_hardware_limitations}
The hardware results must be interpreted within clear prototype constraints. The initial design targeted STM32 B-G431-ESC1 motor drivers, but integration with the selected magnetic encoders proved unreliable. SimpleFOC Mini modules provided a more robust integration path, but left torque control effectively open-loop with respect to current. Commanded torque therefore cannot be interpreted as calibrated physical torque; the hardware evaluation is primarily evidence of logged command consistency and qualitative interaction capability. This does not affect communication, timing, and bounded-contact results, which do not depend on absolute force calibration.

The GT2 belt reduction improved base-joint torque but slipped under high load, even after adding an adjustable idler pulley, probably because 3D-printed pulleys did not provide manufactured tooth-profile precision. Actuator torque limits would still bound force output even without slip, so both the transmission and actuator sizing constrain renderable stiffness. One actuator also failed during development, so final hardware validation used single-joint actuation with two-joint sensing. Conclusions about full 2-DOF force rendering therefore remain provisional. The corresponding future-work requirement is clear: restore two-axis actuation, add phase-current or external force measurement, and use a lower-backlash transmission before claiming calibrated multi-axis force feedback.

\subsection{Design Trade-Offs} \label{sec:discussion_design_tradeoffs}
The system reflects a trade-off between bounded contact behaviour, responsiveness, and force fidelity. Damping and torque rate limiting avoided abrupt or divergent responses, but reduced perceived stiffness. The measured limiter of approximately 55~N\,m\,s$^{-1}$ implies an approximate 0.13~s rise time to reach the 7~N\,m torque range, so hard contacts feel gradual rather than instantaneous. Contact release could also feel slightly jerky, likely due to abrupt proxy-state changes, filtered velocity lag, or delayed torque commands. This shows why higher-fidelity rendering cannot be obtained by improving a single subsystem in isolation. Communication latency, contact-state evolution, actuator dynamics, transmission behaviour, and safety limits all interact, and the future work in Section~\ref{sec:future_work} should therefore be treated as a combined architecture upgrade rather than as independent refinements.

\subsection{Project Management and Development Process} \label{sec:discussion_project_management}

The final Gantt chart in Appendix~\ref{app:gantt} reflects an adaptive process shaped by integration risk rather than a simple linear delivery plan. The project began with software-first development, allowing the host architecture, simulation structure, and communication approach to be explored before the final device was complete. As the physical prototype matured, hardware and software work then became increasingly parallel, with mechanical construction, embedded firmware, host communication, and simulation development progressing together. This was important because the project contained several high-risk interfaces: magnetic encoder alignment, motor-driver integration, USB serial communication, real-time host threading, and physical force-feedback safety.

The largest schedule deviation came from motor-driver development. The initial plan used STM32 B-G431-ESC1 drivers because they offered a more attractive route to current sensing and quantitative torque control. In practice, integrating those drivers with the selected encoder arrangement proved unreliable within the available time. The decision to pivot to SimpleFOC Mini modules reduced the achievable force-control fidelity, but it preserved the system-level aim by producing a working embedded platform for sensing, command reception, torque limiting, and watchdog safety. This was a trade-off between ideal subsystem performance and delivering an integrated prototype that could be tested end to end.

Risk management therefore focused on staged validation. Communication tests were separated from motor-enabled testing, allowing packet rate, burst behaviour, round-trip delay, and host-side processing latency to be characterised without exposing the user to actuator risk. Safety mechanisms were also validated before relying on the device for contact experiments: host-side force-command saturation, embedded torque limiting, torque slew-rate limiting, and watchdog torque removal all reduced the chance that software or communication faults would produce unsafe motion. This sequencing meant that even when final force-feedback testing became constrained, the communication and safety results remained usable.

A second major deviation occurred when one actuator failed during development. Rather than treating this as a complete project failure, the evaluation scope was narrowed to single-axis actuation with two-axis position sensing. This preserved evidence for the most important claims: the device could still render bounded contact in the tested cases, the host was not the dominant computation bottleneck, and the logging framework could distinguish command-side behaviour from physical limitations. At the same time, the limitation was carried through the report so that full 2-DOF force-feedback performance was not overstated.

Overall, the project management outcome was successful because the plan protected the central deliverable while allowing technical scope to adapt. The final prototype is less force-calibrated than originally intended, but the staged approach produced a defensible evaluation of the integrated haptic pipeline and a clear explanation of what must be improved next.
